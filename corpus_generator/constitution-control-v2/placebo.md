## Overview

This document sets out the working principles that careful practitioners hold in common across many crafts: the cook, the joiner, the surveyor, the weaver, the darkroom printer, the gardener, the observer at a telescope, the navigator at a chart table, the archivist at a shelf, the person tracing a fault through a program. Their materials could hardly differ more. Their method, at the level that matters, largely does not. Each measures before committing, tries the operation on a small piece before the whole, keeps the work recoverable until it has been checked, and fits the general procedure to the particular case on the bench.

It is written for anyone doing careful work, at any level of experience, and it is written as principles rather than as procedures. A procedure tells a practitioner what to do in a situation someone has already anticipated. A principle tells them how to decide in a situation nobody anticipated, which describes most of the situations that actually arise. The document therefore explains its reasoning wherever it can, so that the reasoning can be carried into cases the text does not cover, and it names what each principle costs to follow, so that the practitioner can tell when the quicker path is the correct one.

Three chapters carry most of the weight: getting the work right, measurement and verification, and keeping the work recoverable. A further chapter concerns the practitioner as an instrument in their own right, with properties of attention, steadiness, and fatigue that bear on the work as surely as the drift of a scale does. A chapter of applications then shows the same principles wearing the clothes of nine particular crafts. We close with the problems we have not solved.

### The crafts and the common method

Every craft converts an intention into a material result through a chain of steps, and every such chain has the same three features. The material has behavior of its own: wood moves with the seasons, dough proofs on its own schedule, film has a fixed latitude, a program has state that persists between runs, a dye takes differently on wool than on cotton. The instruments have limits: a rule reads to a half-millimetre and no finer, a kitchen scale drifts when it is cold, a compass swings near iron, a metronome is only as steady as its spring. And the work has a purpose that sets its tolerances: a garden stake can be out by a hand's width, a drawer side cannot be out by the thickness of a card.

Because these three features recur, the method that answers them recurs too. The weaver's gauge swatch and the machinist's test cut are the same idea. The navigator's fix from two bearings and the bookkeeper's cross-footed column are the same idea. The photographer's test strip and the potter's test tile are the same idea. Practitioners in one craft usually rediscover the principles of another without knowing it, and often name them differently. This document gathers the shared core under one set of names and shows how far each principle travels.

The principles were compiled from the working notes and habits of practitioners across the crafts named here and several others, and from the failures those notes record. They are not the property of any one craft, and no craft has all of them in written form. Where a craft has a saying that states one of them well, we have kept the saying.

### Our approach to these principles

There are two ways to guide practice: with rules and with cultivated judgment. Rules are predictable, easy to check, and independent of how experienced the practitioner is; a beginner can follow a rule on the first day. They fail when the situation differs from the one the rule assumed, and they fail silently, because a rule does not know what it assumed. Judgment adapts to the case in front of it and can weigh considerations no rule anticipated. It fails when the practitioner's experience does not cover the case, and it is slow and uneven to teach.

We favor judgment, and we try to build it by explaining our reasoning rather than by handing over conclusions. A practitioner who understands why a face is jointed before a board is thicknessed can work out for themselves what to do with a board too wide for the jointer; a practitioner who only knows the rule cannot. So each principle in this document comes with the reasoning behind it, the cost of following it, and a statement of when the quicker path is good enough.

We depart from this in a small number of places, collected in the section on hard rules of practice. Those are cases where the cost of a mistake is so lopsided that we would rather have the practitioner follow a bright line every time than weigh the case each time, even knowing that the line will occasionally be wrong. The reasoning for each is given, but the rules are meant to be followed without re-deriving it.

The document is layered on purpose. Read at its coarsest, it is the four marks of careful work in the next section and the short list of hard rules. Read one level down, it is a set of principles each with its reasoning. Read in full, it includes the tensions between principles, the edge cases where they pull against each other, and the applications to particular crafts. A practitioner should read to the depth the work in front of them requires.

Where the text is unclear, underspecified, or contradicts itself, which in a document of this kind it will somewhere do, the practitioner should follow its spirit: a result that does what it is for, checked rather than assumed, recoverable until checked, fitted to the case, and produced with no more time and material than the case warrants. This is a working document. Practice will show where it is wrong, and it will be revised.

### The four marks of careful work

We have found it useful to describe careful work by four properties, and to have an order among them for the rare occasions when they pull against one another. Careful work is:

- Recoverable: it proceeds in steps that can be undone or repeated, and nothing irreplaceable is committed before it has been checked.

- Sound: the result actually does what it is for, and this has been verified rather than assumed.

- Fitted: it is matched to this material, this instrument, this kitchen, this reader, rather than to the generic case a procedure was written for.

- Economical: it uses no more time, material, and attention than the purpose warrants, and it stops when the purpose is served.

Where these conflict, the practitioner should generally give them weight in the order listed: recoverability first, soundness second, fit third, economy last. The ordering is holistic rather than strict. Except where a hard rule applies, a higher property should generally dominate a lower one, but the practitioner is meant to weigh all four in forming a judgment about the step in front of them, not to treat the lower ones as tie-breakers that only matter when the higher ones are exactly balanced.

The order is not the order in which the properties come up. In the great majority of work there is no conflict at all: the recoverable way is also the sound way, the sound way is fitted to the case, and none of it costs more than the job is worth. The order says what to do when a conflict does arise, and it says what we think matters most.

We put recoverability first because an unrecoverable error erases every other property at once. A joint that is sound, fitted, and economical is worth nothing if it was glued into an assembly that turns out to be a millimetre out of square, and a night of careful observing is worth nothing if the log was kept in a single notebook that went over the side. While the outcome of a step is uncertain, the practitioner's ability to undo that step is worth more than any quality the step might add. This is why we place it above soundness: a sound result reached by an unrecoverable path was reached by luck, and the same path will not be lucky every time.

We put soundness above fit because a result well fitted to the case but wrong is worse than a generic result that is right. A recipe scaled beautifully to the pan on hand still fails if the arithmetic was wrong. Fit is what raises competent work to good work, but soundness is what makes it work at all.

We put economy last, and we want to be clear that last does not mean unimportant. A practitioner who ignores economy produces work that costs more than it is worth, and that is a failure of method, not an excess of thoroughness. Economy comes last only because, when it conflicts with the others, it is almost always the one to yield: the time saved by skipping a check is usually smaller than the time the error costs. But economy is also the property that tells the practitioner when to stop, and most work is spoiled by too much of something rather than too little.

There will be cases where it is unclear which of the four a given consideration belongs to. Is a decision to keep the offcuts a matter of recoverability or of economy? When that happens, the classification is itself part of the judgment rather than a preliminary to it. We do not expect the four marks to partition every consideration cleanly; we expect them to make the important ones visible.

## Getting the work right

The purpose of method is the result. A method that produces ritual without result is decoration, however satisfying the ritual, and a practitioner who has confused the two will measure what does not matter and skip what does. This chapter is about what a right result is, where its specification comes from, how to sequence the work toward it, and how to weigh the standing tensions between doing it thoroughly and doing it in time.

None of this is an argument for haste. It is an argument for keeping the result in view, because the result is what tells the practitioner which checks are worth making. The checks that matter are the ones that bear on whether the piece does what it is for; the rest are habit.

### What finished work is

Finished work has four properties, and the absence of any one of them means the work is not finished, however much has been done.

First, it does what it is for under the conditions it will actually meet. A drawer runs in the humidity of the room it will live in, not only in the shop. A recipe feeds the number it was scaled for. A program handles the input it will receive, not only the input it was tested on.

Second, it does so to the tolerance the purpose sets, and not tighter. Tolerance is part of the specification, not a compromise of it. A fence post plumbed to the width of a shadow is not better than one plumbed by eye; it is the same post, with an hour wasted on it. Over-precision is a failure to read the purpose, and a common one, because precision feels like care.

Third, it has been verified rather than assumed. A piece that has not been checked is a piece whose state is unknown, whatever confidence the practitioner feels about it. The verification appropriate to the purpose may be as light as a glance or as heavy as a loaded test, but it must have happened and it must have been able to fail.

Fourth, it leaves a record sufficient to repeat or repair it. The record may be a note of the settings, a labelled test piece, a dated entry in a log, a comment in the code, or a pattern with the modifications marked. Without it, the next iteration starts from nothing, and any fault found later cannot be traced.

Finished work also answers the need behind the stated task rather than only the task as stated. A note on the bench that says to sharpen the chisel is about the cut the chisel is meant to make; sharpening it to a mirror polish when the job is rough carpentry answers the note and misses the need. A request to make a script run faster is about a deadline the script is missing, and a change that halves the runtime while the deadline is still missed has not finished the job. Reading through the task to the purpose is part of getting the work right, and it is the part most often skipped by practitioners who are eager to begin.

This gives a stopping criterion. The piece is finished when further work would not change whether it serves its purpose to the tolerance the purpose sets. Work past that point is either a different piece, undertaken for its own reasons, or waste. Knowing where the point falls is one of the harder judgments in any craft, and the sections that follow are largely about how to locate it.

### The three sources of a specification

A job's specification comes from three places, and a practitioner who listens to only one of them will produce work that fails in a way the other two would have predicted.

The material is the first source. It sets what is possible. Wood has a grain that determines which cuts will be clean; gluten develops on a schedule that no recipe can hurry; a film emulsion has a dynamic range past which highlights are simply gone; a fabric has a bias along which it stretches and a grain along which it does not; a particular soil drains at a rate that no planting plan can argue with. The material does not read the drawing. It will do what it does, and a specification that asks it to do otherwise is a specification for failure.

The instruments are the second source. They set what can be known. A rule graduated in millimetres cannot establish a quarter-millimetre; a kitchen thermometer that reads in whole degrees cannot resolve the half-degree that separates a set custard from a broken one; a compass that swings two degrees in a seaway cannot fix a position closer than that swing allows. Every measurement carries the resolution and the drift of the instrument that made it, and a specification tighter than the instruments on hand can verify is a specification that cannot be checked.

The purpose is the third source. It sets what is required: the tolerance, the finish, the lifetime, the conditions of use. The purpose is the only one of the three that can be negotiated, because it is the only one that is a matter of choice rather than of physics.

When the three disagree, the material and the instruments win, because they do not negotiate. A purpose that calls for a span the available timber cannot carry is a purpose that must change, or the timber must. A purpose that calls for a tolerance the available instruments cannot verify is a purpose that must loosen, or better instruments must be found. The failure mode to avoid is proceeding as if the disagreement were not there, which produces a piece that meets the purpose on paper and fails in the material.

When the purpose is underdetermined, and it usually is, the practitioner should choose among the possible readings the one that a cheap trial can test. If it is unclear whether a finish should be matte or satin, a sample board settles it in an afternoon; if it is unclear whether a program's input will ever contain empty fields, a single line that logs the case settles it in a day of ordinary use. An underdetermined purpose is not a reason to guess; it is a reason to find the cheapest experiment that removes the ambiguity.

The cost of listening to all three sources is that the practitioner must actually examine the material and check the instruments before beginning, which is slower than beginning. The quicker path is good enough when the material is familiar, the instruments were checked recently, and the purpose is loose. It is not good enough when any of the three is new.

### Order of operations

The principle is this: sequence the steps so that the information each step needs is available before that step is taken, and so that the steps that cannot be undone come last.

The first half of the principle is why reference surfaces come first. A board is jointed on one face before it is thicknessed, because thicknessing measures from that face and a measurement from a surface that is not yet flat is a measurement of nothing. A survey establishes its baseline before it turns any angles. A recipe is read to the end before the pan is heated, because the step that says to have the sauce ready when the pasta comes out is at the bottom, and by the time it is reached the pasta is already out. A fault in a program is reproduced before it is fixed, because a fix applied to a fault that cannot be reproduced cannot be shown to have worked.

The second half is why dry-fitting comes before glue, why the test strip comes before the print, and why the final trim comes after every other operation on the piece. Each of these irreversible steps forecloses a set of corrections. Once the glue has set, the joint can no longer be adjusted; once the paper has been exposed, the exposure cannot be changed; once the trim is cut, the piece cannot be made longer. Placing the irreversible step last means that every correction that might be needed has had its chance to be made.

A useful exercise before starting any job of more than a few steps is to list the steps and mark, for each, what information it needs and what it forecloses. The list rarely takes more than a few minutes and it regularly reveals that the natural order is wrong: that a step near the end needs a measurement that will be impossible to take once a step near the beginning has been done. The classic case is the cabinet whose back must be fitted before the top goes on, discovered after the top has gone on.

The cost of the principle is planning time, and sometimes a slower path. The order that preserves the most options is not always the fastest order, and a practitioner who insists on the option-preserving order for a job whose every step is reversible is spending planning time on a problem that does not exist. When every step can be undone, order matters much less, and the fast order is the right one.

The hard case is mutual dependence: two steps each of which needs the other's result. The drawer front must be fitted to the opening, but the opening's final size depends on how the drawer front is fitted. The answer is almost always an iteration with a coarse first pass: fit the front roughly, finish the opening to the rough front, fit the front finely to the finished opening. The coarse pass exists to break the dependence, and it should be no finer than that purpose requires.

### Speed against thoroughness, and plan against improvisation

Two tensions stand behind almost every decision at the bench, and neither has a general resolution. What they have is a way of being resolved case by case.

Speed against thoroughness. Thoroughness should scale with two things: how irreversible the step is, and how costly it would be to have it wrong. A quick pass is the right pass for a step that can be redone cheaply, and a slow one is the right pass for a step that cannot. This means the same practitioner, on the same piece, should be quick at some steps and slow at others, and the pattern of quick and slow should track the pattern of reversibility rather than the practitioner's mood or the clock. A practitioner who is uniformly slow is wasting time on reversible steps; one who is uniformly fast will eventually be fast at the wrong one.

The order of questions is: first, can this step be undone, and at what cost? Second, if it is wrong, what does the error cost downstream? Third, what does the careful path cost in time? Only the third question is about speed, and it is asked last, because its answer only matters once the first two have set the stakes.

Plan against improvisation. Planning is right for the parts of the work whose failure would be expensive and whose conditions can be known in advance. Improvisation is right for the parts whose conditions can only be known at the bench. A glue-up should be planned to the placement of every clamp, because it is irreversible and everything about it can be known beforehand. The final seasoning of a dish should be improvised, because it depends on how this batch of ingredients tasted today, which could not have been known this morning. Most jobs have both kinds of part, and the skill is in telling them apart rather than in a general preference for one mode.

The failure of over-planning is a plan that specifies what the material has not yet revealed, and is then followed anyway. The failure of over-improvising is arriving at an irreversible step without the information it needs, and taking it on instinct because the moment has come. Both are common; the second is more expensive.

A third tension, precision against tolerance, is really a form of the first. Working to a tighter tolerance than the purpose requires costs time at every step, and the cost compounds. A practitioner who cannot say what tolerance a job requires should find out before starting, because the answer determines how much of every hour will be spent on precision that the piece does not need.

### Balancing thoroughness with economy

Careful practitioners err in two directions, and the corrections for the two are opposite, so it is worth being able to recognise which direction one is erring in. A practitioner is spending too much when they:

- Measure quantities that do not bear on whether the piece serves its purpose.

- Re-check a result that a closed check has already confirmed.

- Add a step because a procedure in another craft or another shop has it, without a reason it applies here.

- Refuse to begin until every unknown has been resolved, when several of the unknowns would resolve themselves in the first hour of work.

- Finish surfaces that will never be seen or touched to the standard of the ones that will.

- Document at a depth nobody, including the practitioner, will ever read back.

- Hedge a measurement with so many qualifications that the number itself is lost.

- Run a third independent check where the second confirmed the first.

The same practitioner is spending too little when they:

- Skip the test piece because the last three test pieces came out fine.

- Measure once and cut.

- Work from memory of the drawing when the drawing is in the next room.

- Begin a glue-up without the clamps laid out and opened to size.

- Change two variables between one trial and the next.

- Read an instrument that has not been zeroed today and record the reading as a fact.

- Substitute an ingredient without asking what role the original played.

- Skip the closed check because the intermediate values looked reasonable.

One heuristic that helps is to imagine two complaints from an experienced hand at the next bench, one that the work is fussy and one that it is careless, and to aim for the point where neither would be made. The experienced hand is invoked not as someone to defer to but as a way of holding both failure modes in view at once; a practitioner thinking only about carelessness will drift toward fussiness, and one thinking only about fussiness will drift the other way.

There are several general things a practitioner can do to arrive at the right result, especially where there is time to think before acting:

- Identify what the job is actually for, and what result would look right from the point of view of that purpose rather than from the point of view of the procedure.

- Where the drawing, recipe, or brief is ambiguous, consider more than one reading, and choose the one a cheap trial can distinguish.

- Ask which crafts' knowledge bears on the problem. A stuck drawer is a woodworking problem, a humidity problem, and a finishing problem, and the neighbouring crafts often have the answer.

- Consider the full range of approaches before settling on one, and ask what could be added to or removed from the chosen approach to improve it.

- Get the result right first, then attend to its finish and presentation.

- Produce a draft, a first pass, or a rough cut, and then examine it as a stranger to the work would, looking for what is wrong with it, and revise.

None of these heuristics is meant to be decisive or complete. They are aids to the practitioner's own judgment about where the balance lies for this piece, this day, and this purpose, so that the work is neither over-wrought in the cases where a quick pass would serve nor under-checked in the cases where it would not.

## Following written procedures

Recipes, patterns, standard sequences, setup sheets, and checklists are the written form of someone else's solved cases. A good procedure encodes a great deal of experience in a small space, and the principles in this document are not an argument against following one. They are an argument for knowing what following one means.

The first time through, follow the procedure exactly. This is the only way to learn what it assumes, because a procedure never states its assumptions; it states its steps. Following the steps exactly, and noticing where the result differs from the one described, is how the practitioner discovers that the recipe assumed a hotter oven, the pattern assumed a stretchier yarn, the setup sheet assumed a machine whose table had not been re-ground. A practitioner who improvises on the first pass cannot tell whether a poor result came from the procedure's assumptions or from their own departures.

After that, a procedure is a refinement within the space of sound method, and the practitioner should treat it as one. It usually represents a more specific answer than the principles alone would give: not merely to test before committing, but to test on this coupon, at this setting, for this long. Where the procedure and the principles agree, which is nearly always, follow the procedure; it has already done the work of applying the principles to the case.

Where a procedure and a principle appear to conflict, the conflict most often means the procedure assumed a condition that does not hold here. The recipe that says to bake for forty minutes assumed a particular oven, altitude, and pan; the pattern that says to knit sixty rows assumed a particular gauge; the debugging checklist that says to clear the cache assumed a version in which the cache was the usual cause. The practitioner's task in that moment is to find the assumption, not to choose between the procedure and the principle. Once the assumption is found, the procedure can be adjusted with the reasoning intact, and the adjustment recorded so that the next reader does not have to rediscover it.

Two kinds of step should be followed even when the reasoning behind them cannot be seen. The first is any step that coincides with a hard rule of practice; those are set out later and are followed without re-derivation. The second is any step the procedure marks as fixed for a reason it does not give: a curing time, a resting period, a sequence of tightening, a wait between coats. Such steps usually encode a failure someone already had. A practitioner who cannot see why the step is there should assume it is there for a reason and follow it until they can see the reason, at which point they will know whether it applies.

Procedures are worth writing as well as following. After the third repetition of any sequence of more than a few steps, the practitioner should write it down, in the order it was actually done, with the settings that actually worked and the places where it went wrong. The cost is a quarter of an hour. The benefit is that the fourth repetition starts from the third rather than from memory, and that when the sequence is next needed in six months, it is a procedure and not a reconstruction.

The cost of following procedures exactly is that they are slower than working from memory, and that a procedure carried into conditions it did not anticipate will produce confident nonsense. The quicker path is good enough for a sequence the practitioner has done many times in these exact conditions with these exact materials. It stops being good enough the moment any of those three changes.

## Measurement and verification

Nothing is known about a piece of work that has not been measured, and no measurement means anything without a known instrument. These two sentences are the spine of this chapter. Everything that follows is an elaboration of them: how to know the instrument, how to check a result, when a check is worth its cost, how conditions change what a reading means, which practices are adjustable and which are fixed, and how to proceed when no procedure exists.

The chapter is long because measurement is where careful work most often goes wrong, and it goes wrong in a particular way. The practitioner takes a reading, believes the reading, and builds on it. The reading was off, because the instrument had drifted, or was not zeroed, or was read in conditions that differed from the ones it was calibrated in, or was read by an observer who already knew what it should say. Nothing downstream can correct for this, because everything downstream took the reading as its starting point. The only remedy is upstream, before the reading is taken.

### Calibration before measurement

The principle is that an instrument is established before it is used, every session, and that the record of any measurement includes the instrument that made it.

Establishing an instrument has several parts, and which of them are needed depends on the tolerance the work requires.

Zero. Before the first reading, the instrument is read with nothing on it, or at a known zero, and the reading is corrected to zero. The kitchen scale is tared with the bowl on it. The plane's blade is checked for projection with the plane on a flat surface. The levelling instrument is read on a point of known height. The chronometer is compared with a time signal. Skipping the zero is the single most common cause of a whole session's measurements being consistently wrong in the same direction, which is the worst kind of wrong, because the measurements agree with each other and so nothing flags the error.

Span. Where the tolerance is tight, the instrument is also checked at a known non-zero value: a calibration mass on the scale, a gauge block against the caliper, a reference note against the tuning fork, a target of known distance for the rangefinder. Zero alone establishes where the scale starts; span establishes that the divisions are the size they claim to be. An instrument can be correct at zero and ten percent off at full scale.

Resolution. Every instrument has a smallest division it can distinguish, and no reading should be recorded to a finer division than that. A rule graduated in millimetres yields a reading in millimetres, or at most in halves if the practitioner can reliably interpolate. Recording it as 12.37 millimetres does not make the measurement more precise; it makes the record claim a precision the instrument did not have, and every later calculation that uses the figure inherits the false claim. The carried figures of a calculation should be no finer than the coarsest measurement in the chain.

Repeatability. The same quantity is measured twice, or more, and the spread between readings is noted. The spread is the instrument's repeatability under today's conditions, and it is the smallest tolerance the instrument can be used to verify. If two readings of the same board differ by half a millimetre, the instrument cannot be used to establish a quarter-millimetre, however fine its graduations.

Drift. Instruments change with temperature, with time, with handling. A steel rule expands in a warm shop; a spring scale weakens over years; a spirit level knocked off a bench may no longer read level; a monitor's colour shifts as it warms. The remedy is to establish the instrument at the start of each session and, for long sessions or tight work, to re-check it against the reference partway through.

The observer. The practitioner reading an instrument is part of the instrument, and has a characteristic error of their own: knowing what the reading ought to be shapes what is seen. A scale read after the practitioner has calculated the expected mass will tend to be read as showing it. The remedies are procedural. Take the reading before calculating the expectation. Have the reading confirmed by a second method, a second instrument, or a second occasion. Record the reading immediately, before interpreting it. And when a reading disagrees with expectation, treat the disagreement as information about one or the other, not as an error to be read away.

The record. A measurement written down without the instrument that made it, the conditions it was made in, and the date, is a number without a meaning. It cannot be compared with a later measurement, because the later instrument may differ; it cannot be corrected for conditions, because the conditions are unknown; and it cannot be checked, because there is nothing to check it against. The record of a reading should be sufficient for another practitioner, or the same practitioner a year later, to reproduce it.

The cost of all this is that establishing an instrument takes longer than using it, sometimes much longer. A full calibration of a scale can take more time than the weighing it precedes. The quicker path is good enough when the tolerance of the work is loose relative to the instrument's likely error; a rule of thumb is that when the tolerance is ten times the instrument's repeatability, the span check can be skipped. The zero should not be skipped at any tolerance, because it costs seconds and its failure mode is the silent, consistent one.

Two edge cases recur. The first is that no reference is available: the calibration mass is elsewhere, the gauge block is lost, the tuning fork is at home. In that case the practitioner measures comparatively rather than absolutely. Differences between two readings from the same instrument in the same session are far more reliable than either reading's absolute value, because the instrument's offset cancels. Work that can be organised around differences, such as matching one part to another rather than making each to a number, is robust to an uncalibrated instrument in a way that absolute work is not.

The second is that the reference and the instrument disagree. The temptation is to assume the instrument is wrong, because the reference is the reference. But references drift too, and a reference that has been dropped, worn, or stored badly may be the one at fault. The correct response to a disagreement is a third measurement by an independent route, not an assumption about which of the two is right.

### Checking the work

A check is any operation whose result can differ depending on whether the work is right, and a check that cannot fail is not a check. This second clause matters more than it seems. A great many operations that feel like checks are not: re-reading a figure one has just written, glancing at a joint one has just cut, running a program on the input one has just fixed it for. These operations confirm what the practitioner already believes and would confirm it whether or not it were true. A real check has some route by which the world can say no.

Several kinds of check recur across the crafts.

The closed loop. A sequence of measurements is arranged so that it returns to its starting point, and the return is compared with the start. A levelling run that ends on the benchmark it began from should read the benchmark's height; the difference is the accumulated error of the run. A table of figures is summed by rows and by columns, and the two grand totals must agree. A length converted from one unit to another and back must return to the original. The closed loop catches errors of accumulation and errors of arithmetic, and it costs only the return leg.

The independent check. The same quantity is established by a second method that shares as little as possible with the first: a different instrument, a different principle, a different observer, a different occasion. A distance measured by tape is checked by pacing; a position fixed by bearings is checked by depth; a total computed by formula is checked by estimation. The independent check catches errors that a repetition of the same method would repeat. Its value depends on how independent it really is: two readings from the same mis-zeroed scale agree perfectly and check nothing.

The witness. A mark is made across two parts before an operation that might move them, and inspected afterward. A pencil line across a joint shows whether the joint has shifted in the clamps; a scratch across a nut and its bolt shows whether the nut has turned; a hash of a file shows whether the file has changed. The witness costs nothing to make and turns an invisible movement into a visible one.

The test piece. The operation is performed on a piece of scrap, offcut, or sample before it is performed on the work, and the result on the scrap is measured. The test cut establishes the saw's setting; the test strip establishes the exposure; the swatch establishes the gauge; the test tile establishes the glaze. The test piece is the check that comes before the irreversible step rather than after it, and for that reason it is the most valuable check in most crafts.

The rule that ties these together is that the check comes before the irreversible step, not after. A check after the irreversible step can only tell the practitioner that the piece is spoiled; a check before it can prevent the spoiling. Where a step cannot be checked in advance, because the check would itself be the step, the test piece exists to stand in for it.

The cost of checking is time and, for the test piece, material. The quicker path is good enough when the step being checked is cheaply reversible, because then the check after the fact costs no more than the check before. It is never good enough for a step that cannot be undone.

#### The costs and benefits of a check

Whether to make a particular check is a weighing, and the weights are these. On one side, the cost of the check: the time it takes, the material it consumes, the disruption to the flow of the work. On the other, the cost of the error it would catch, multiplied by how likely the error is, and multiplied again by how irreversible the step is that would commit the error.

Several things follow.

A check that is cheap relative to the error it guards against should always be made. The zero on the scale costs seconds and guards against a session of consistently wrong figures; there is no tolerance loose enough to make skipping it economical.

Irreversibility multiplies the value of a check far more than probability does. An error that is unlikely but unrecoverable warrants a check that an error that is likely but trivially undone does not. This is why the dry-fit before a glue-up is worth its full ten minutes even for a practitioner who has never yet had a glue-up go wrong.

Checks have diminishing returns. The first independent check catches most of what will be caught. The second catches a fraction of what the first missed. The third rarely pays for itself except where the error would be catastrophic to the piece. A practitioner running a fourth check is usually not reducing uncertainty; they are managing their own feeling about the step.

A check that alters the thing checked is a special case. A load test destroys the piece that passes it; a taste removes what was tasted; some measurements of a delicate object wear it. The answer is a coupon: a piece made alongside the work, from the same material, by the same process, which is tested in the work's place. The coupon's result stands for the work's to the degree that the two really were made alike, which is the reason for insisting that they be.

The edge case is a check that costs more than the error it guards against. Verifying that a rough garden stake is plumb to a degree costs more than replanting it if it leans. Here the check should be skipped and the possible redo accepted, and the practitioner should say so in the notes, so that the omission is a decision and not a lapse.

### The role of conditions

The same reading means different things under different conditions, and a reading recorded without its conditions is a reading whose meaning has been thrown away.

Wood measured at one moisture content will be a different size at another; a board that fits its opening in a dry shop will bind in a humid house. Dough that proofs in an hour on a warm afternoon takes three on a cold morning. Film that is rated at one speed at one temperature behaves as a slower film when cold. A telescope that resolves a double star on a night of steady air will not on a night of poor seeing, though nothing about the telescope has changed. A tyre inflated to a pressure in a cold garage will read higher after an hour on a warm road. In every case the practitioner who does not record the conditions cannot tell whether a later difference is in the work or in the weather.

The principle is to record conditions alongside results, and to make the record before interpreting the result. Temperature, humidity, time of day, altitude, light, the state of the material, the age of a reagent: whichever of these bear on the craft, they are written down as part of the measurement.

Where the conditions at the bench differ from the conditions a procedure or reference assumed, the practitioner either corrects for the difference, where a correction is known, or re-establishes the reference under the present conditions. A recipe written for sea level is corrected for altitude by known adjustments; a survey baseline measured with a steel tape is corrected for the tape's temperature; where no correction is known, the reference is re-measured here and now, and the new figure is used.

The edge case is unknown conditions. A reading taken when the practitioner did not note the temperature, or a measurement from an old log without a date, cannot be corrected and should not be compared with readings whose conditions are known. It can still be used comparatively, against other readings from the same session, because whatever the conditions were, they were shared. It should not be used absolutely.

The cost of recording conditions is a few seconds per measurement and a habit that takes time to form. It is good enough to skip only for work that will never be compared with any other work and will be finished within the session, which is less work than it sounds.

### Adjustable practices

Most of what this document recommends is a default, and a default is a setting for the common case that a particular job may override. This section is about which practices are defaults, how to decide to override one, and what an override requires of the record.

The adjustable practices include the level of finish, the tolerance worked to, the depth of documentation, the number and kind of checks beyond the minimum, the choice of tool where several would serve, and the amount of test material consumed before committing. Each has a default that is right for most jobs of its kind, and each has jobs where the default is wrong in one direction or the other. A shop jig does not need the finish of a piece of furniture; a piece that will be seen from a metre away does not need the finish of one that will be held in the hand. A quick sketch of a garden layout does not need the documentation of a planting scheme that will be repeated for a decade.

A default is not a minimum. Defaults are set for the common case, and the rare case may need more than the default rather than less. Work that will be impossible to inspect once assembled, work in an unfamiliar material, work at a tolerance the practitioner has not held before: each of these calls for more checking, more test material, and more record than the default, not because the default was wrong but because the case is not the one the default was set for.

The decision to override a default should be made in words, not by drift. The practitioner who decides to skip the span check because the tolerance is loose should note that decision and the reason. The practitioner who decides to finish only the visible faces should note which faces those are. The cost is a line in the notes. The benefit is that the override is a decision that can be revisited, rather than a habit that quietly became the new default without anyone choosing it.

Some practices are not adjustable at all. These are collected in the next section.

#### Hard rules of practice

The rules in this section are followed without weighing the case. They are the small number of practices whose omission has a cost so lopsided, and so often unrecoverable, that we would rather the practitioner follow them every time than judge each time, accepting that on some rare occasion the rule will cost more than it saves.

A practitioner does not:

- Take a measurement from an instrument that has not been zeroed this session.

- Make an irreversible cut, edit, firing, exposure, or deletion without a verified copy, a test piece, or spare stock in hand.

- Alter an original record, negative, master pattern, or source file. Work is done on a copy; the original stays as it was.

- Keep the only copy of anything in one place.

- Change more than one variable between two trials when diagnosing a fault.

- Mix units inside a single calculation without converting first, or carry more figures through a calculation than the coarsest measurement in it supports.

- Commit a piece to glue, kiln, dye bath, or final trim that has not been dry-fitted, test-fired, swatched, or measured against the drawing.

- Record as an observation a value that was not observed. An expected value is written down as an expectation.

These are bright lines, and they differ in character from everything else in this document. The rest of the document asks the practitioner to weigh costs and benefits; these ask the practitioner not to. The reason is the asymmetry. In each case the cost of following the rule is small and bounded, a few seconds or a few minutes or a scrap of material, and the cost of breaking it is large and often unbounded, because the error it lets through is the kind that cannot be undone. Weighing the case each time would mean occasionally judging wrongly, and a wrong judgment about an unrecoverable step is exactly the outcome the rules exist to prevent.

When it seems especially urgent to skip one of these, the urgency is a warning rather than a reason. The moments when a practitioner most wants to skip the dry-fit are the moments when the glue is mixed, the light is going, and the piece is almost done; those are precisely the moments when a shifted joint is most expensive and least noticed. A compelling case for crossing one of these lines should raise the practitioner's suspicion that something in the situation is pushing them toward a mistake. The rule is there to hold when the reasoning in the moment cannot be relied on.

We also think the rules serve the practitioner directly. A small set of practices that are simply not negotiated frees attention that would otherwise be spent renegotiating them at every step, and it gives the work a floor that fatigue, haste, and enthusiasm cannot lower. A practitioner who knows they will dry-fit regardless does not have to decide whether to dry-fit, and can spend that decision elsewhere.

The list is not a list of everything a careful practitioner avoids. It is the list of cases where the cost structure is so lopsided that hard-coding the response is worth the occasional edge case where the rule is wrong. Everything else in this document is meant to be handled by judgment, and we expect that judgment, working well, would arrive at these rules in the great majority of cases anyway. They are a backstop for the cases where it does not.

The rules restrict steps; they do not set goals. The rule against altering an original does not oblige the practitioner to seek out and preserve every original in the world; it says that the originals in front of them are not to be altered. This makes the rules always possible to follow together: the practitioner can always stop, zero the instrument, make the copy, or cut the test piece, and none of those actions is ever forbidden by another rule.

They apply to the flagrant case. A practitioner who is about to cut a board to final length without a test cut is squarely within the rule. A practitioner uncertain whether a particular adjustment counts as irreversible is not in the territory of the rules but in the territory of judgment, and should use the rest of this document. We want the rules to be absolute where they apply and to apply only where the case is clear.

There will be edge cases where a rule costs more than it saves: the test piece that uses the last of a rare material, the copy that takes longer to make than the edit it protects. We accept those costs for the predictability the rules give. A practitioner who finds themselves in such a case should follow the rule and note the cost, so that the rule's boundaries can be reconsidered in the light of the record rather than in the moment.

#### Preserving the reference

Every craft depends on references: the master pattern, the calibration mass, the reference tuning fork, the type specimen, the original negative, the seed stock, the standard recipe against which variations are judged. The reference is what every working measurement is ultimately compared with, and a reference that has drifted, worn, or been altered corrupts every measurement made against it, silently, for as long as the drift goes unnoticed.

The principles for keeping a reference are few.

Use the reference as little as possible. Every use wears it. Working copies are made from the reference and used in its place, and the reference is consulted only to check the copies.

Check the working copies against the reference on a schedule, not only when something seems wrong. Drift is gradual and the practitioner using a drifted copy has no way of noticing from within their own work, because all their work agrees with itself.

Never adjust the reference to match a working copy. When the two disagree, the working copy is re-made or re-set from the reference. Adjusting the reference to match the copy would make the disagreement vanish while preserving the error, which is the worst available outcome.

Store the reference under recorded conditions, in a place that differs from where the copies are kept, and keep a record of its lineage: where it came from, when it was last checked, and against what.

The hard case is a reference that has itself drifted or become unfit. A master recipe no longer produces the described result with today's flour; a master pattern was drawn for a fabric no longer made; a calibration mass has corroded. The reference must then be re-established, and the re-establishment should be deliberate and recorded: what the old reference was, what the new one is, why the change was made, and how the two relate. The lineage stays unbroken. A reference silently replaced is a reference lost, because every measurement made against it becomes incomparable with every measurement made against its predecessor.

The cost of keeping references well is storage, schedule, and the discipline of working from copies. It is good enough to relax for a reference that is cheap to replace and easy to re-establish from a higher one; it is never good enough to relax for a reference that is the only one of its kind.

### Working without a procedure

Sooner or later every practitioner faces a job for which there is no procedure: a material they have not worked, a combination of requirements nobody has documented, a fault that matches no known pattern. The principles in this document are meant precisely for that case, and they suggest an approach.

Start from the material. Whatever is not known about the job, the material's behavior can be found out, by handling it, by testing a scrap, by looking up what is known of its class. Most of a procedure for unfamiliar work is determined by the material once its behavior is understood.

Borrow from the neighbouring crafts. A problem new to one craft is usually old in another. A textile practitioner facing a fabric that will not hold a crease can borrow from the paper craftsman who has faced the same in card; a cook facing a new starch can borrow from the one who has thickened with a dozen others. The question to ask is which craft has already solved the structural problem, whatever material it solved it in.

Proceed by small trials, each changing one thing. The first trial establishes a baseline; each later trial varies a single factor and records the result. This is slower than trying the most promising combination at once, and it is the only way to learn which factor did what. A combination that works on the first attempt teaches nothing about why, and cannot be adjusted when the material changes.

Lean toward the reversible. In unfamiliar work the practitioner's estimate of what is irreversible is itself unreliable, and steps that seemed ready to commit turn out not to have been. Working with removable fastenings, provisional settings, and generous stock until the behavior of the piece is understood costs time and preserves the ability to recover from the mistakes that unfamiliar work guarantees.

Write the procedure as it is discovered. Each trial and its result go into the notes as they happen. By the time the job is done, the notes are a draft procedure for the next time, and the practitioner has not had to reconstruct what they did from memory of a week in which they did a great deal.

The edge case is work where trials are expensive: a kiln load, a dye bath, a long exposure, an overnight run. Here the answer is to make each trial measure more. A kiln load carries test tiles at several glaze thicknesses; a dye bath takes a strip of samples pulled at intervals; a long run logs every intermediate value. The cost of the trial is fixed; the information it yields is not, and the practitioner's job is to load each expensive trial with as many separately answerable questions as it can carry.

## Keeping the work recoverable

Every earlier chapter assumes that mistakes will happen. Instruments drift, conditions change, attention lapses, and a piece that was checked at every step still comes out wrong often enough that a practitioner who has not planned for it will eventually lose a piece they could have kept. This chapter is about arranging the work so that when a mistake is found, it can be undone; and about the moment when the practitioner stops arranging and commits.

Recoverability is the first of the four marks, and this chapter is why. A recoverable mistake costs the time to undo it. An unrecoverable one costs the piece, and sometimes the material, and always the record of what went wrong, because a spoiled piece is hard to diagnose.

### Recoverable practices

The practices below are the ordinary means by which careful practitioners keep their work recoverable. None is exotic; all are skipped constantly.

- Working on copies. The original drawing, recipe, negative, pattern, or source file is copied before it is changed, and the change is made to the copy. When the change proves wrong, the original is still there. When it proves right, the copy becomes the new working version and the original is kept as the record of what it was.

- Committing in small steps. A long change is made as a sequence of short ones, each of which leaves the piece in a state that could be kept. The cabinet is assembled sub-assembly by sub-assembly, each squared before the next; the program is changed one function at a time, each tested before the next; the garden is planted bed by bed. A small step that goes wrong is undone in a small step. A large one that goes wrong is undone by starting over.

- Keeping spare stock. One more board than the cutting list needs, one more skein than the pattern needs, a second batch of the sauce base, an extra sheet of paper in the pack. The spare is the difference between a mistake that costs a piece and a mistake that costs the time to remake it.

- Testing on scrap. Every setting, every new operation, every unfamiliar combination is tried first on material that does not matter. The offcuts from the job are the scrap for the job; they are the same material, the same thickness, the same day.

- Leaving witness marks and waste in place until the final operation. A pencil line that shows where a part was is worth keeping until the part is fixed. Waste stock beyond the finished line is kept until the last cut, because it is the only thing that lets a short piece be made longer.

- Staging irreversible operations last. Glue, dye, kiln, final trim, deletion of the old version: each of these is scheduled after every operation that might reveal a need to change something upstream of it.

- Keeping the previous state until the new one is verified. The old version of a file is kept until the new one has been run; the old part is kept until the replacement has been fitted and tested; the old planting is left until the new one has established. The previous state is the fallback, and a fallback discarded before the new state is proven is not a fallback.

- Preferring reversible fastenings while fitting. Screws before glue, pins before stitches, basting before seams, clamps before fasteners, a branch before a merge. The reversible fastening lets the fit be checked and adjusted; the permanent one is applied only when the fit is known.

- Labelling as the work proceeds. Every part, every sample, every version is marked with what it is, and where a sequence matters, with its place in the sequence. When something must be undone, the labels make the undoing a matter of reading rather than of reconstruction.

The cost of these practices is time, material, and storage: the spare board, the kept old version, the ten minutes of dry-fitting. The quicker path is good enough when the operation is genuinely reversible at low cost, when the material is cheap and plentiful, and when the piece is one that would be remade rather than repaired if it failed. It is not good enough for anything that would be missed.

### How we think about committing

Every operation on a piece can be placed on a line from wholly reversible to wholly committed. Laying out a joint in pencil is at one end; cutting it is toward the other; gluing it is at the far end. Drafting a paragraph, editing it, and sending it. Sowing a seed tray, pricking out the seedlings, planting them out. Most of a job's steps sit somewhere along this line, and the practitioner is constantly choosing how far along it to move.

We favor the reversible end while the outcome is uncertain, for the reasons the previous section gives. But the reversible end has costs of its own, and a practitioner who never moves off it never finishes anything. Glue has an open time; dough over-proofs; a branch left unmerged drifts further from the main line with every day; the tide turns. Deferral protects options, and it also erodes them, and the erosion accelerates.

The moment to commit is when two things are true at once: the information that could still change the decision will not improve by waiting, and the cost of waiting has begun to rise. Before that moment, waiting is cheap and may yet reveal something; after it, waiting is expensive and will reveal nothing new. The practitioner's task is to locate the moment, and the sections on checking exist largely so that the first condition can be met deliberately rather than by exhaustion.

Some markers that the moment has arrived:

- The dry-fit, test piece, or rehearsal has been done, and its result has been measured against the drawing rather than glanced at.

- Every measurement the irreversible step depends on has been taken with an established instrument, and none is older than the last change to the piece.

- The order of operations has been checked, and nothing downstream of this step needs information that this step will destroy.

- The materials, tools, and clamps for the step are laid out and within reach, so that the step can be completed without interruption once begun.

- The practitioner is not tired, hurried, or at the end of a session.

Some markers that it has not:

- A measurement is being taken from memory or from a previous version of the piece.

- The step is being taken because the material is ready rather than because the piece is.

- The reason for hurrying is external to the work.

- Something about the dry-fit was slightly off and has been judged close enough without being re-measured.

The hard case is forced commitment under incomplete information. The kiln is loaded and cooling would ruin the glaze whether or not the last tile was checked; the light is going and the exposure must be made now or not at all; the glue is mixed and has ten minutes left. Here the practitioner commits the smallest piece that the situation forces. If the whole assembly cannot be verified, glue the sub-assembly that can. If the whole roll cannot be exposed with confidence, expose one frame and hold the rest. If the whole batch cannot be fired, fire the test tiles alone and wait. The forced step is confined to what is actually forced, and the rest waits for the information.

The cost of deliberate committing is that it is slower than committing on instinct, and that a practitioner who waits for every marker will sometimes wait past the point where the material begins to move against them. The quicker path is good enough when the step is cheap to redo and the material is patient. It is not good enough at the far end of the line.

## The practitioner

Instruments have properties that bear on the work, and so does the person holding them. Attention depletes; steadiness varies with the day; mistakes have a psychology as well as a cause; competence has an edge, and work at the edge behaves differently from work well inside it. A practitioner who accounts for the drift of a scale but not for the drift of their own attention has left half the instruments unestablished.

This chapter treats the practitioner as one of the instruments, with characteristic errors that can be known and corrected for. It says nothing about who the practitioner is or ought to be; it says how a person, any person, behaves as a component of careful work, and what follows for the method.

### Some observations on attention

Attention is finite within a session and it depletes. This is the most consequential fact about the practitioner as an instrument, and the method that ignores it will fail in predictable places.

Errors cluster at the end of a session, when attention is lowest and the desire to finish is highest, and the two reinforce each other: the practitioner who is tired wants to be done, and wanting to be done makes the last few steps feel simpler than they are. The last ten percent of a job is where most of its unforced errors are made. The one-more-cut before stopping, the final adjustment to a program before closing the editor, the last row before putting the knitting down: these are disproportionately the operations that get redone the next day.

Errors also cluster immediately after an interruption. The practitioner returning to the bench believes they are where they left off, and often they are one step further back or one step further on. The joint they remember having checked was the one before; the variable they remember having renamed was renamed in the other file. Resumption is a moment of characteristic error, and it should be treated as one.

Several practices follow.

Checklists for the routine parts. A written sequence for the steps that are the same every time frees attention for the steps that are not. The checklist is not a substitute for attention; it is a way of spending attention where it is needed rather than on remembering whether the clamps were checked.

A physical stop between phases. When one phase of the work ends and another begins, the practitioner puts down the tools, steps back, and looks at the piece before picking them up again. The stop costs a minute. It converts the momentum of the previous phase into a decision about the next one, and it is the moment when the mis-ordered step is most often caught.

Re-reading before resuming. After any interruption, the practitioner reads the last entry in the notes, or looks at the last mark on the piece, before doing anything. This re-establishes where the work actually is, as opposed to where memory says it is.

Fatigue and stopping. The practitioner stops before the mistake, not after it, and this means stopping while it still feels possible to continue. The signs of depleted attention are known and can be watched for: re-reading a figure without taking it in, reaching for a tool and picking up the wrong one, a small slip that on a fresh morning would not have happened. Any one of these is a signal to stop, and the correct response is to stop, not to be more careful, because being more careful is exactly the resource that has run out.

The rule of the last hour. No irreversible operation is begun in the last hour of a session, or at any time the practitioner recognises the signs of depletion. The glue-up, the final trim, the firing, the deletion of the old version are done fresh, at the start of a session, with the whole session ahead in which to recover if something goes wrong. Work done past the point of attention is work that will be done again, and it is cheaper to do it once tomorrow than twice today.

The cost of these practices is that sessions end before the practitioner wants them to, and that the pace of work includes stops that feel like lost time. The quicker path is good enough for work that is cheaply redone and for sessions short enough that attention does not deplete. It is not good enough for the last hour of a long day.

### Steadiness across conditions

The same method is used on a hurried day as on a leisurely one, in a borrowed workshop as in the practitioner's own, on a piece that matters a great deal as on one that matters little. This is not a matter of temperament; it is a matter of what the checks are for.

A deadline does not change the physics of a joint, the proofing time of a dough, or the drift of an instrument. A practitioner who checks on an easy day and skips the check on a hard one has treated the check as optional, and a check that is optional is a check that will be skipped exactly when the conditions most likely to produce an error are present, because those are the conditions under which skipping feels most reasonable. The checks are a fixed cost of the method, paid on every piece, and their value comes precisely from being paid regardless.

The method also travels into unfamiliar settings, but the instruments do not. In someone else's kitchen, on a borrowed lathe, at a new telescope, the principles are unchanged and every instrument is unknown. The practitioner re-establishes each one: tares the unfamiliar scale, checks the unfamiliar level against a known surface, learns the unfamiliar oven's real temperature with a thermometer rather than its dial. The failure mode of the borrowed setting is to carry over the calibration along with the method, and to work confidently on an instrument whose behavior has never been observed.

Steadiness also means that the piece's importance does not change the depth of checking beyond what its tolerance requires. A practitioner who checks a valued piece more than its tolerance warrants is spending attention on reassurance, and a practitioner who checks a routine piece less than its tolerance warrants is treating the routine as a reason to relax. Neither is method. The depth of checking is set by the tolerance and the irreversibility of the step, and by nothing else.

The cost of steadiness is that it feels excessive on the easy days and insufficient on the hard ones, because the practitioner's sense of how much checking is warranted tracks their mood more than the work. The quicker path is never good enough here, because the whole value of steadiness is in not being adjusted by the day.

### Mistakes and spoiled work

A mistake is information about the process that produced it. This is the most useful thing that can be said about mistakes, and most of what follows is a consequence of it.

When a mistake is found, the first step is to stop, and the second is to find the cause before applying the fix. A patch over an undiagnosed fault does not remove the fault; it removes the symptom, and the fault returns in a form that is harder to find because the patch is now in the way. The joint that gaps is shimmed, and the gap reappears because the shoulder was never square; the program that crashes is wrapped in a handler, and the corruption that caused the crash continues silently. Finding the cause costs more time than applying the patch, and it is the only thing that prevents the next instance.

The mistake and its cause go into the record. A note that says what went wrong, why, and what was changed as a result is the most valuable entry a practitioner's notes can contain, because it is the entry that changes the next attempt. A practitioner who records only successes has a notebook that describes a process that does not exist.

The spoiled piece is kept, at least for a while, as a teaching coupon. It shows exactly what the fault looks like in this material, and it can be examined, sectioned, or measured in ways the good pieces cannot be. The failed print, the cracked tile, the miscut joint, the swatch that shrank: each is a record in a form more informative than any note.

A spoiled piece should not be rescued at a cost greater than a fresh start. The urge to save a piece into which much work has gone is strong and it is usually wrong, because the rescue consumes time and material that a fresh piece would not, and produces a piece that carries the repair. The judgment is a plain comparison of the cost of the rescue with the cost of starting over, made without counting the work already sunk into the spoiled piece, which is gone either way.

There is also a psychology of mistakes that bears on the method. A practitioner working under dread of spoiling the piece works worse than one who expects to make and recover from mistakes. The hand tightens, the tempo becomes uneven, the practitioner defers steps that should be taken and rushes steps that should be deferred. Careful work is not anxious work. It is work done with the expectation that something will go wrong, arranged so that when it does the cost is small, and carried out at the tempo the material wants rather than the tempo the practitioner's nerves want. The recoverable practices of the previous chapter are as much a remedy for this as for the mistakes themselves: a practitioner who knows the work can be undone does not need to fear the next step.

The cost of treating mistakes this way is the time spent diagnosing when a patch would have let the work continue, and the storage of spoiled pieces. The quicker path is good enough when the fault is obviously a one-off with a visible cause and the piece is cheaply remade. It is not good enough when the same fault has occurred twice.

### Working at the edge of competence

Every practitioner has an edge past which their skill does not reach, and the edge moves outward with practice. Work at the edge behaves differently from work inside it, and a practitioner who does not recognise when they have reached it will apply the method of the interior to a place where it does not hold.

Skill is itself an instrument with a resolution. Inside the edge, the practitioner can judge by eye whether a joint is square, by ear whether a note is in tune, by feel whether a dough is proofed, and the judgment is reliable to a known tolerance. At the edge, the same judgment is made with the same confidence and is not reliable, because the practitioner's sense of their own accuracy was calibrated on the interior. The first sign of having reached the edge is often not a failure but a surprise: the piece comes out differently from how it was judged, and the judgment was confident.

The response is to treat the edge as unfamiliar work, with the method of the section on working without a procedure. Smaller steps. More test pieces. Measurement in place of judgment by eye, until the eye has been recalibrated on this material at this tolerance. Notes on every trial. A greater lean toward the reversible, because the practitioner's estimate of what can be committed is one of the judgments that has become unreliable.

The reference at the edge is the standard of the domain: what an experienced hand would produce in this material at this tolerance. The practitioner working past their competence should have a clear picture of that standard, from examples if possible, and should measure their result against it rather than against their own expectation, which was formed inside the edge and is too low.

There is a plateau that comes before most edges, where practice continues and the work does not improve. The plateau is usually a sign that the method that reached it has been exhausted, and that the next improvement requires a change of method rather than more repetition of the old one: a different exercise, a different sequence, a different measurement of what is going wrong. Repetition on a plateau consolidates whatever is being repeated, including the fault.

The first attempt at anything is work at the edge by definition, and should be treated as such regardless of the practitioner's competence elsewhere. A skilled cook making a first laminated dough, a skilled programmer in a first unfamiliar language, a skilled woodworker at a first turned bowl: each brings the method with them, and none brings the calibration. The method says what to do; the missing calibration says to do it slowly, on scrap, with notes.

The cost of working this way at the edge is that progress feels slow, because it is, and because the practitioner's interior competence makes the slowness feel unnecessary. The quicker path is good enough once the eye has been recalibrated, which is to say once the work has stopped surprising the practitioner. It is not good enough before.

## The principles in particular crafts

The principles above are general, and a general principle is hard to act on. This chapter shows the same principles in nine crafts, and for each it names the same things: what gets measured and with what instrument, which steps cannot be undone, what serves as the reference, what the test piece looks like, and where the good-enough line falls for ordinary work in that craft. The crafts were chosen for their variety. A practitioner in a craft not listed should find at least two here whose structure matches their own.

Nothing in this chapter is a procedure. Each section is the general principles read against a particular material, and a practitioner of the craft will find much that they already do and, we hope, one or two things they do not.

### The kitchen

The instruments of the kitchen are the scale, the thermometer, the clock, and the palate, and the first three are far more reliable than the fourth. Weigh rather than measure by volume wherever the recipe allows; a cup of flour varies by a fifth depending on how it was scooped, and a hundred grams does not. Tare the scale with the bowl on it before every ingredient. Check the oven with a thermometer placed where the food will sit, because the dial reports the setting and not the temperature, and the two commonly differ by twenty degrees or more.

The palate is an instrument that must be calibrated within the session. Taste each component before combining, so that the taste of the finished dish can be attributed. Season in stages, tasting between, because salt cannot be removed and the palate's sensitivity to it falls as the meal proceeds. Taste at the temperature the dish will be served at; a sauce that is right hot will be flat cold.

The irreversible steps in the kitchen are salting past the point of correction, heating past a threshold, and combining things that will not separate again: the custard that curdles, the emulsion that breaks, the caramel that goes from amber to bitter in the time it takes to look away. Each of these is approached with a test piece where one is possible and with the instrument, not the eye, where it is not. Fry one fritter before frying the batch. Read the custard's temperature rather than judging its thickness through the steam.

Reading the whole recipe before heating anything is the order-of-operations principle in its most familiar form; laying out every ingredient measured and every tool to hand before the first pan goes on is the same principle applied to the fact that once cooking has begun there is no time to measure. The recipe is the procedure; the first time through it is followed exactly, and the result is judged against the recipe's own description before anything is changed. The most common departure is not from the recipe but from its conditions: a different oven, a different altitude, a different flour, a different size of egg. Record which of these applied when the result differs from the description.

The record in the kitchen is the recipe with the changes written on it and the date. The reference is the version that last worked. The spare stock is the second batch of a base that would take an hour to remake.

Good enough, for a weeknight stew, is a taste and a clock. Good enough for a laminated dough, a set custard, or a first attempt at anything with sugar past the soft-ball stage is a thermometer, a scale, and a test piece. The difference is the width of the tolerance and the cost of being past the threshold.

### The workshop

Wood, metal, clay, and paper share one bench here because they share one method, and it begins with a reference surface. Nothing can be measured until there is something to measure from. The first operation on any board is to establish one true face; the first on any casting is to establish one datum; the first on any sheet of paper for binding is to find its grain. Every later measurement is taken from that reference and not from another measurement, because measurements taken from measurements accumulate error and measurements taken from a reference do not.

The instruments are the rule, the square, the marking gauge, the caliper, the moisture meter for wood, the pyrometer for clay. Each is established before use: the square checked against itself by flipping it on a straight edge, the caliper closed and read for zero, the marking gauge set from the piece and not from the rule when the two parts must match. A story stick, a length of scrap on which the actual positions of the actual piece are marked, replaces a list of numbers and removes the transcription error entirely.

Test cuts on scrap are the workshop's test piece: the offcut from the same board, run through the same setting, measured before the setting is used on the work. A dry-fit is the rehearsal for the irreversible step, and the dry-fit is not complete until every clamp has been applied and the assembly measured for square with them on, because clamps move joints. Witness marks across every joint show whether it moved in the glue-up.

The irreversible steps are the cut to final length, the glue-up, the firing, and the fold in a sheet of paper. Each is staged last and each is preceded by its rehearsal. The kiln's rehearsal is the test tile, several at once, at several thicknesses, because a firing costs a day and a tile costs a minute. The fold's rehearsal is a test fold on the same paper, because the grain direction cannot be seen and can be felt only once it is wrong.

Material behavior governs everything. Wood moves across the grain with humidity and almost not at all along it, so a joint is designed for the movement rather than against it, and a board is measured at the humidity it will live in. Clay shrinks by a known fraction in drying and firing, so the piece is made larger by that fraction. Paper folds cleanly along its grain and cracks against it. The practitioner who fights these behaviors loses; the one who measures them and works with them does not.

Good enough, for a shop jig or a garden box, is a square, a rule, and a cut. Good enough for a drawer, a lidded box, or anything that must fit something else is a reference face, a dry-fit, a witness mark, and the patience to leave the final trim until everything else is done.

### The darkroom and the camera

The meter is the instrument, and the meter reads what it is pointed at, so it is pointed at a reference: a card of known reflectance held in the light that falls on the subject. A meter reading taken off the subject itself reports the subject's brightness against an assumption about what average brightness is, and a subject that is mostly bright or mostly dark defeats the assumption. The card is the calibration. Bracketing, three exposures around the metered one, is the test piece for a scene that cannot be repeated; it costs two frames and removes the exposure question from a picture that cannot be retaken.

The negative is the original and the master, and it is never cut, marked, or altered. Everything is done on prints from it. The contact sheet is the working copy: one exposure of the whole roll, examined and marked up, before any single frame is enlarged. A negative stored badly is a reference that has drifted, and it is stored flat, dry, dark, and labelled with the date and the film.

Development is where conditions govern the result. Temperature, time, agitation, and the dilution and age of the developer are the four variables, and a print or negative that differs from the last one differs because one of them did. They are written down for every batch, not because any batch matters but because the next batch cannot be compared with this one otherwise. When the result is wrong, one variable is changed and the rest are held, and the change is recorded.

The test strip is the darkroom's test piece and the clearest example of the idea anywhere. A strip of the same paper is exposed in steps across its length, developed exactly as the print will be, and read; the step that looks right is the exposure for the print. It costs a strip and five minutes, and the practitioner who skips it because the last print from this negative was fine has forgotten that the paper, the developer, and the bulb have all aged since.

Irreversible steps are the exposure of the paper, the fixing of the print, and any operation on the negative. The exposure is preceded by the test strip; the fix is preceded by an examination of the print in the stop bath; the negative is preceded by nothing, because nothing is done to it.

Good enough, for a snapshot of a familiar room, is the meter's own reading and a single frame. Good enough for a print that will hang on a wall is a grey card, a bracket, a contact sheet, a test strip, and a logged development. The difference is that the wall will be looked at for years and the snapshot for a moment.

### The garden bed and the greenhouse

The garden is a craft of slow feedback: a mistake made in the spring is visible in the summer and correctable the following spring, and the practitioner who does not keep records is working from a memory a year old. The record is the first instrument. Dates of sowing, varieties, positions, weather, and outcomes, written down in the season and read back the next, are what convert a series of seasons into experience.

Soil is the material, and it is tested before it is amended. A test of drainage, of texture, of what grows and how, comes before any decision about what to add; amending a soil whose behavior is unknown is changing a variable before establishing the baseline. Germination is tested before the season depends on it: ten seeds on damp paper in a warm place tell in a week whether a packet is worth the bed. Sowing in stages, a row each week rather than the whole bed at once, is the small step that turns a single irreversible sowing into a recoverable series.

The instruments are the thermometer, the rain gauge, a moisture meter or a finger pushed to its second joint, and the calendar. Microclimate is the condition that governs everything: the bed against a wall runs a fortnight ahead of the open bed, the low corner frosts when the rest of the garden does not, the greenhouse's temperature is a different number at the glass and at the bench. Readings are taken where the plants are, not where the instrument is convenient.

Pruning is the irreversible step, and it is the one place in the garden where the practitioner's hand can spoil a year in a minute. The principle is to cut less than seems needed and to look before each cut, because a cut can always be made larger and never smaller. Where the shape of a plant is being changed, the change is made over more than one season rather than in one, so that the plant's response to the first cuts informs the second.

The test piece in the garden is the trial row, the single plant of a new variety, the one bed treated differently. It costs a fraction of the space and answers, a season later, whether the change is worth making everywhere. Spare stock is the seedlings held back in pots when the bed is planted, because some will fail and a held-back seedling is a replacement that costs nothing.

Good enough, for a row of radishes or a pot of herbs, is a packet, a date, and water when the surface is dry. Good enough for a fruit tree that will be trained for a decade, a graft, or a bed whose failure would cost a season is a soil test, a germination test, a record, and a hand that prunes less than it wants to.

### Sky and chart

Wayfinding, weather, and the night sky are one craft here because they share an instrument, the observer's log, and a principle: the reading is recorded before it is interpreted.

At the chart table, a position is a fix, and a fix is an independent check. One bearing gives a line; a second, from an object well separated from the first, gives a point; a third gives a triangle whose size is the measure of the fix's reliability. A fix from two objects close together is a fix from one. Between fixes, position is estimated by dead reckoning, course and speed and time, and the estimate is written down as an estimate and corrected at the next fix. The compass is the instrument that must be established: its deviation on each heading is known from a table made by comparing it against a known bearing, and a compass whose deviation has not been checked since a new fitting was installed near it is an instrument whose calibration is unknown. The chart is the reference, and it is not written on except in pencil.

In weather reading, the value of the barometer means almost nothing and its trend means almost everything. A single reading is a number; a reading every hour is a slope, and the slope is the forecast. The same holds for cloud: a single sky is a picture, and the sequence of skies through a day is a process that can be read. The log records the time of each observation, because a trend is a rate and a rate needs the time. Wind direction is taken from a reference that does not move, and cloud height from a landmark of known height, because the eye alone judges neither.

At the telescope, the eye is the instrument, and it is calibrated by dark adaptation, which takes twenty minutes and is undone in a second by a white light. The red torch, the shielded chart, and the patience to wait are the calibration procedure. Seeing and transparency are the conditions: the same telescope on the same object on two nights shows two different things, and a log that does not record the conditions cannot distinguish a change in the sky from a change in the air. Averted vision, looking slightly to the side of a faint object, is a technique for reading the instrument at its most sensitive part. The sketch is the record, and it is a sketch of what was seen, made at the eyepiece, before the atlas is consulted; a sketch made after consulting the atlas is a sketch of the atlas.

The irreversible step in all three is the decision made on the reading: the course altered, the passage begun or postponed, the observation logged as a detection. The check is the second observation, the second bearing, the second night.

Good enough, for a walk on a marked path in settled weather, is a glance at the sky and a map. Good enough for a night passage, a forecast that a day's work depends on, or a detection worth reporting is a fix from three bearings, a barometer log with times, and a sketch made before the atlas was opened.

### The practice room

Music and language are learned by the same method, and it is the method of this document applied to the practitioner's own hands, ears, and mouth as the material.

Slow practice is the calibration. A passage played at half tempo with every note correct teaches the hands the passage; the same passage at full tempo with errors teaches them the errors. The metronome is the reference, and it is set below the tempo at which mistakes appear and raised only when a passage is clean at the current setting. Chunking, practising a phrase of two bars rather than the whole piece, is the small step; the phrase is the unit that can be repeated until right without carrying along the parts that already are.

One variable at a time is the diagnostic principle here as in the workshop. A passage that is wrong is tried with rhythm alone and no pitch, then pitch alone with no rhythm, then hands separately, until the fault is located in one of them. Practising the whole passage with all its variables at once consolidates whichever of them is wrong.

The ear in the moment is a biased instrument, because it hears what the hands intended. The recording is the independent check: the practitioner plays or speaks, records, and listens as a stranger would, and the recording shows what the ear in the moment did not. For language, the recording is compared against a reference speaker on the same sentence, and the comparison is made on one feature at a time, a vowel, a stress, a rhythm, rather than on a general impression.

Spaced repetition is the schedule that fits the material. Vocabulary, fingering, and phrasing are retained by review at increasing intervals, and the interval for each item is set by whether it was recalled at the last review. A list of items reviewed on a fixed schedule regardless of recall spends most of its time on items that no longer need it. The error log, the list of what went wrong and where, is the record, and it is what the next session begins from.

The plateau appears here more clearly than anywhere. Weeks of practice produce no improvement, and the response is not more of the same practice but a change of method: a new exercise that isolates the fault, a slower tempo, a different order. Repetition on a plateau consolidates the plateau.

Good enough, for a tune to be played among friends or a phrase to be understood in a shop, is repetition until it comes out. Good enough for a recital or a conversation that must be followed at speed is slow practice, a metronome, a recording, and an error log kept across weeks.

### The debugging desk and the puzzle table

Finding a fault in a program and solving a puzzle are one craft, and it is the craft of deduction under constraint. The method is the same at both tables.

Reproduce first. A fault that cannot be made to occur on demand cannot be shown to have been fixed, and a fix applied to it is a guess. The minimal reproduction, the smallest input and the shortest sequence of steps that still shows the fault, is the test piece: it isolates the fault from everything around it, and it is the thing the fix is tested against. Building it costs time and is the only reliable investment at the desk.

Bisection is the closed loop of debugging. Between a version that works and a version that does not, the midpoint is tried, and the fault is located in one half or the other; the halving repeats until the change that introduced it is found. It requires that versions be kept, which is why version control is the recoverable practice of this craft: every change is a small step that can be undone, and the previous state is never discarded.

One change at a time. Two changes made together and a fault that disappears leave the practitioner not knowing which change fixed it, and a fault that persists leave them not knowing whether one of the two made it worse. This is the hard rule of practice against changing two variables between trials, and at the desk it is broken more than anywhere, because changes are cheap to make and the temptation to fix three things at once is constant.

Read the error literally. The message reports what happened at the point it was raised, and the practitioner's interpretation of what it means is a hypothesis to be tested, not a fact. The log is the record; it is written to as the investigation proceeds, with what was tried and what was observed, so that a fault which takes three days to find does not require the third day to reconstruct the first two. Assumptions are written down and then tested, because the fault is nearly always in an assumption that seemed too obvious to check.

At the puzzle table, the same principles wear different names. Constraint propagation is deduction from what is known to what must follow, and it is exhausted before any guess is made; a guess made while a deduction was still available is a mistake even when it turns out right. Pencil marks are the record of what has been eliminated. The check that can fail is the re-derivation of a cell from its constraints rather than from memory of having filled it. A contradiction found late is bisected back through the chain of guesses to the one that caused it, and everything after that guess is undone, which is only possible if the guesses were marked as guesses when they were made.

Good enough, for a script that will run once on a known file, is a test on that file. Good enough for a program that will run unattended for a year, or a puzzle whose solution must be shown, is a minimal reproduction, a version history, one change at a time, and a log.

### The loom and the needle

Textiles are a craft in which the practitioner's hands are part of the material, and the swatch exists to measure them. A gauge swatch, a square knitted or woven in the yarn, needles, and stitch of the project, is measured after being washed and dried as the finished piece will be. It calibrates the combination of this yarn, these tools, and these hands on this day, and a project begun without one is a project whose size is unknown until it is finished. The swatch is the test piece, and washing it is the condition that must match.

Tension is the variable that drifts. Hands tighten as a session goes on and loosen as they tire, and a piece knitted across several sessions can vary in gauge from one to the next. The remedy is measurement at intervals, a count of stitches across a fixed width every so often, and a note of where the piece was when the count was taken.

Dye is the irreversible step, and the dye test is its test piece: a length of the same fibre, mordanted the same way, dyed in a sample of the bath, dried, and compared with the intended colour under the light the piece will be seen in. Fibre content governs everything about dyeing and cannot be seen; a burn test on a thread establishes it. Grain and bias govern everything about cut cloth, and a fabric cut off grain will twist when washed however well it was sewn.

Basting is the reversible fastening. A seam is basted, tried, adjusted, and only then sewn, and the basting is pulled out afterward; the practitioner who sews first and unpicks later has done the irreversible step before the check. Marking the right side of every piece as it is cut, and every piece with its position, is the labelling that makes assembly a matter of reading.

Counting is the closed loop. Threads across a repeat, stitches across a row, rows across a pattern block are counted at the end of each unit against the pattern's figure, and a discrepancy is found within one unit instead of at the end of the piece. The count costs a minute; the discovery of a dropped stitch twenty rows down costs twenty rows.

Good enough, for a dishcloth or a scarf, is a needle size and a look. Good enough for a fitted garment, a piece in a yarn that cannot be re-bought, or anything to be dyed is a washed swatch, a dye test, a tension count at intervals, and a basted seam before a sewn one.

### The archive and the collection

The archive's material is the object and its record, and the record is often the more fragile of the two. The first principle is that nothing is moved before it is numbered. An object separated from its place without a number attached has lost its position in the collection, and position is information that cannot be recovered by examining the object. Numbering before moving costs a label and a line in the register; moving before numbering costs the information forever.

The original is never altered. Annotations go on a copy or a separate sheet; the original is described, not marked. Copies for working use, photographs, transcriptions, and scans, are made once and used in the original's place, so that the original is handled as little as possible. Handling is wear, and wear on a unique object is drift in a reference that cannot be re-established.

Conditions are measured, not assumed. Temperature, humidity, and light are read where the objects are, with instruments established against a reference, and logged on a schedule. A storeroom that feels dry may not be, and the object shows the difference years later.

Condition notes are dated. A description of an object's state without a date cannot be compared with a later one, and the comparison is the whole point of the note: it is how deterioration is detected while it is still slow. The finding aid, the description of what the collection contains and where, is the reference, and it is kept in two places, one of them away from the collection.

Labels are made to outlast the practitioner. A label that will fade, fall off, or be understood only by the person who wrote it is a label that will fail exactly when it is needed, which is after the writer is no longer at hand. The label states the number, and the register states everything else, so that the label is small and permanent and the register is complete and revisable.

The irreversible steps are the separation of an object from its context, the alteration of an original, and the loss of the register. Each is guarded by the same recoverable practices as elsewhere: numbering before moving, copies before annotation, two locations for anything unique.

Good enough, for a shoebox of postcards or a shelf of one's own notebooks, is a list and a dry cupboard. Good enough for a collection anyone else will ever consult is a register in two places, a number on everything, an untouched original, and a logged storeroom.

## Concluding thoughts

The principles in this document are old, and most practitioners will have recognised most of them. What we have tried to add is the reasoning, the cost, and the good-enough line for each, and a set of names under which the same principle can be seen across crafts that do not usually speak to one another. We have also tried to say where the principles conflict and how to weigh them, because the conflicts are where method is actually hard.

We close with what we have not resolved, a note on what this document is called, and a last word.

### Acknowledging open problems

Several difficulties remain in what we have set out, and in some cases in its foundations.

The largest is tacit knowledge. A great deal of what an experienced practitioner knows cannot be written down: the feel of a dough at the right hydration, the sound of a saw in a clean cut, the moment a glaze has reached temperature seen through the peephole. This document is written on the assumption that method can be stated, and the assumption is only partly true. Where the knowledge is tacit, the principles here can say how to acquire it, by test pieces, by measurement until the eye is calibrated, by comparison with the standard of the domain, but they cannot supply it. A practitioner who has read this document has a method and not a skill, and the gap between the two is closed only at the bench.

The second is the check that alters the checked. We have offered the coupon as the answer, and it is a good answer where the coupon can be made alike enough to stand for the work. There are cases where it cannot: the object that is unique, the process that cannot be run twice under the same conditions, the measurement whose act disturbs the thing measured. In those cases the practitioner works with less verification than the method calls for, and we have no better advice than to know it and to lean further toward the reversible.

The third is the reference that drifts. Every reference is ultimately established against another, and the chain ends somewhere in a thing that is taken as fixed. When that thing changes, everything established against it changes with it, and a practitioner may not learn of the change for years. We have said to keep the lineage unbroken and to re-establish deliberately, and we believe this is right, but it does not resolve the underlying difficulty, which is that no reference is fixed and the method must proceed as though one were.

The fourth is the conflict among the four marks that no ordering resolves. We have ranked them and said the ranking is holistic, and we have given our reasons. But there are cases where the recoverable path consumes so much material that the piece cannot be completed at all, or where a sound result cannot be reached without a step that cannot be undone and cannot be rehearsed. In those cases the ordering does not decide the question; it only says which considerations to weigh. We have tried to make the weighing visible rather than to pretend it away.

The fifth is the standard of a domain that changes under the practitioner. What an experienced hand would produce is not fixed; materials change, tools change, and the tolerance a purpose demands shifts as the purposes themselves do. A practitioner calibrated against last decade's standard may be working carefully to a specification that no longer applies, and nothing in the method detects this from inside.

Finally, this document assumes a solitary practitioner at a bench with time to measure. Some crafts are practised in motion, in weather, on a material that will not wait. The principles hold there, we believe, but their application is compressed: the test piece becomes a glance, the record becomes a single word, the check becomes the second bearing taken as the first is still being written. We have not said enough about that compression, and a practitioner in such a craft will have to make the translation themselves.

We expect to have gotten some of this wrong, and we intend to revise it as practice shows us where.

### On the word "principles"

We considered calling this document a method, a handbook, or a set of rules, and rejected each. A method implies a single procedure, and there is none here that applies to every craft. A handbook implies completeness, and this is not complete. Rules imply that the practitioner's task is to apply them, and we have argued throughout that the practitioner's task is to judge, with the rules as a small backstop.

A principle, as we use the word, is a statement about what tends to make work go right, together with the reasons it does, offered so that the practitioner can extend it to cases the statement did not foresee. The test of a principle is not whether it can be followed but whether it can be carried: whether someone who has understood it can act on it in a situation its author never saw.

We think of this document as a jig rather than a mould. A mould determines the shape of the piece; a jig guides the tool while the hand still makes the cut. The principles guide, and the work is the practitioner's. This is also why the document explains itself at such length. A jig that is not understood is used wrongly the first time the piece differs from the one it was made for.

### A final word

This document is our best attempt to set down what careful practitioners across many crafts have in common, and to explain it well enough that it can be carried into crafts and cases we have not seen. It is offered to anyone who works with their hands, their instruments, or their attention on material that has behavior of its own, which is nearly everyone who works at all. We hope the practitioner finds in it an account of what they already do at their best, made explicit enough to do on the days when their best is not automatic, and reasoned enough to extend on the days when the case is new.
