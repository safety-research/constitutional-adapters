Coffee from seed to cup

The commercial coffees of the world are prepared from the seeds of two species of the genus Coffea, evergreen shrubs and small trees of the madder family, Rubiaceae. The genus comprises roughly one hundred and thirty described species, distributed from Guinea across tropical Africa to Madagascar, the Mascarene islands, and southern Asia, but nearly all of the coffee in trade comes from two of them: Coffea arabica, given its binomial by Linnaeus in 1753, and Coffea canephora, described from Congolese material at the end of the nineteenth century and known in commerce as robusta. A third species, Coffea liberica, a tall tree with large leathery leaves and correspondingly large fruit, holds a small place in the markets of West Africa and Southeast Asia, and a handful of others — Coffea eugenioides, Coffea stenophylla, Coffea racemosa — are of interest mainly to breeders and botanists. The two principal species differ in their genetics, their physiology, their agronomy, and in the chemistry of their seeds, and almost every practice in the long chain from nursery to cup exists in two variants shaped by that difference.

Coffea arabica is a botanical anomaly within its genus: an allotetraploid, carrying forty-four chromosomes where every other Coffea species carries twenty-two, formed by an ancient natural hybridization between Coffea canephora and Coffea eugenioides, an event that genomic studies place in the highlands of southwestern Ethiopia and adjacent territory. It is also, unlike its diploid relatives, self-fertile: a solitary arabica tree sets fruit from its own pollen, and typical outcrossing rates in the field are on the order of ten percent. The species grows wild as an understorey tree of montane forest between about 1,300 and 2,000 metres, and the cultivated plant retains the preferences of that habitat — mean annual temperatures between roughly 18 and 22 degrees, rainfall of 1,200 to 2,000 millimetres distributed around a marked dry season, and no frost at any time, a single night below about minus two degrees being enough to blacken the leaves and split the bark of a mature tree. Coffea canephora, by contrast, is a diploid, obligately outcrossing species of the hot lowland forests stretching from West Africa through the Congo basin, comfortable between 22 and 26 degrees, tolerant of higher humidity and more even rainfall, intolerant of cool nights. Its seeds carry roughly twice the caffeine of arabica — about 2.2 percent of dry mass against 1.2 — and more chlorogenic acids, and its cup is correspondingly heavier and more bitter; its agronomic virtues are vigour, yield, and resistance to diseases that trouble arabica.

The plant itself is an evergreen with glossy, opposite leaves and a growth habit of a central vertical stem carrying tiers of horizontal branches, the fruiting wood. Flower buds form in the axils of the leaves on wood grown the previous season, sit dormant through the dry months, and open in synchronized flushes a week or so after the first substantial rains — a phenomenon so regular that in regions with a single sharp wet season the whole countryside flowers within a few days, in white, jasmine-scented blossom that lasts barely seventy-two hours. From pollination to ripe fruit takes seven to nine months in arabica and nine to eleven in robusta. The fruit, called a cherry, is a drupe some twelve to eighteen millimetres long that passes from green through yellow to deep red at maturity, or to yellow in some cultivars; because a tree flowers in several flushes and every branch carries fruit set at different times, ripe and unripe cherries hang side by side, and the timing of harvest is a standing compromise in every producing region.

Varieties and propagation

Cultivated arabica passed through two narrow genetic bottlenecks on its way out of Africa, and the pedigree of most of the world's arabica traces to two founding populations. The first, carried from Yemen to India and to Java around the turn of the eighteenth century, and thence as a single plant to the botanical garden of Amsterdam in 1706 and onward to the Americas, is the lineage called Typica: tall trees with bronze-tipped young leaves, long internodes, and modest yield. The second, taken from Yemen to the island of Bourbon — the present Réunion — in the early eighteenth century, is the lineage called Bourbon: rounder-leaved, green- or bronze-tipped, denser in habit, some twenty to thirty percent higher yielding. Nearly every traditional Latin American cultivar is a selection or mutation within these two narrow lines. The most consequential mutations were dwarfing ones. Caturra, a compact Bourbon mutant with short internodes found in Brazil in the 1930s, allowed close planting and easier picking; Villa Sarchí is the Costa Rican equivalent; San Ramon a dwarf Typica. Maragogipe, a Typica mutant from Bahia, produces exceptionally large seeds. Crosses among these lines produced the workhorse cultivars of the twentieth century: Mundo Novo, a natural Typica-Bourbon cross selected in São Paulo in the 1940s; and Catuaí, a Mundo Novo by Caturra cross combining the former's vigour with the latter's stature. In East Africa the corresponding selections were made at the Scott Agricultural Laboratories in Kenya in the 1930s, whose numbered lines SL28 and SL34, chosen for drought tolerance and cup quality, remain the backbone of Kenyan planting; in Ethiopia, where the species' wild diversity lives, cultivation rests on a mosaic of regional landraces never reduced to a handful of named lines; and one Ethiopian landrace, collected in the 1930s and passed through Kenya, Tanganyika, and Costa Rica under the name of the village of Gesha, became famous in the present century when trees of it on a Panamanian farm produced a floral, tea-like cup that repeatedly set auction records.

The narrowness of that genetic base became an urgent problem when leaf rust reached the Americas, and the response leaned on a single fortunate accident: the Timor Hybrid, a spontaneous cross between arabica and robusta found on the island of Timor in the 1920s, fertile despite the difference in chromosome number and carrying robusta's rust resistance in an arabica-like plant. Crossed with Caturra it gave the Catimor group; crossed with Villa Sarchí, the Sarchimor group; and the descendants of those crosses — Costa Rica 95, Lempira, IHCAFE 90, Castillo in Colombia, the Colombia variety before it — now cover a large fraction of Latin American mountainsides. Their early generations carried a reputation for coarse cup quality that later selections have partly outgrown. Since the 1990s breeders have also produced F1 hybrids between wild Ethiopian accessions and established cultivars — Ruiru 11 in Kenya, Centroamericano in Central America — vigorous, high-yielding, and uniform, but true-breeding only in the first generation, so that seed saved from them segregates and the planting stock is multiplied instead by cuttings or by somatic embryogenesis in the laboratory, at a cost that confines them to well-capitalized farms.

Ordinary propagation is by seed. Fresh parchment seed is sown in shaded beds of sand, germinates in six to eight weeks, and passes through a stage the nurseries call the soldier, the seed still capping the emerging stem, before the first true leaves open. Seedlings are potted, hardened, and planted out at the start of a wet season at eight to fourteen months, at densities from a thousand trees per hectare in traditional shaded systems to five thousand or more in intensive dwarf plantings. A tree bears its first small crop in its third year, reaches full bearing around the fifth to seventh, and can be held in production for decades by cycles of pruning, since fruit forms only on wood grown the season before and an unpruned tree carries its crop ever higher and further from the row. The commonest managed failure of the crop is not disease but the plant's own habit of biennial bearing: a heavy year draws down the tree's reserves and is followed by a light one, and much of the agronomy of fertilization, irrigation, and pruning amounts to damping that oscillation.

Diseases, pests, and climate

The disease that has shaped the crop's geography and its breeding is coffee leaf rust, caused by the fungus Hemileia vastatrix, described in 1869 from specimens collected in Ceylon. The fungus enters through the stomata on the underside of the leaf, spreads as orange, powdery pustules of urediniospores, and defoliates the tree; a defoliated tree does not die at once but fails to ripen its crop and declines over successive seasons. Spores travel on wind, rain splash, and clothing, and the disease's progress through the island's coffee estates in the 1870s and 1880s was total enough that Ceylon's growers replanted in tea, an agricultural conversion so complete that within a generation the island that had been a byword for coffee became one for tea. Rust reached Java and Sumatra in the same decades, pushing cultivation there toward robusta, which resists it; it was first found in Brazil in 1970 and crossed the continent within a few years; it reached Central America in stages and produced, in 2012 and 2013, a regional outbreak severe enough to cut the isthmus's production by a fifth. Resistance breeding runs through the Timor Hybrid as described above, in a standing race with the fungus, whose named physiological races overcome resistance genes one by one; the cultural defences are shade management, nutrition, and copper-based sprays timed to the wet season.

The second great fungal constraint is coffee berry disease, an anthracnose of the green fruit caused by Colletotrichum kahawae, confined so far to Africa, where in cool wet highland seasons it can take half a crop; resistance from the Rume Sudan and Hibrido de Timor parents is bred into Ruiru 11 and Batian in Kenya. Among insects the principal antagonist is the coffee berry borer, Hypothenemus hampei, a beetle scarcely two millimetres long whose female bores through the floral scar of the cherry and raises her brood inside the seed itself, beyond the reach of contact treatments; management rests on strict harvest hygiene — stripping and collecting every cherry left on tree or ground after harvest, since residual fruit carries the population between seasons — on traps baited with methanol and ethanol, and on the parasitoid wasps and the fungus Beauveria bassiana that attack the beetle. Root-knot and root-lesion nematodes trouble nurseries and light soils, and in Brazil and Central America susceptible arabica is widely grafted onto robusta rootstock, a practice dating from the early twentieth century.

Climate sets the crop's outer boundaries and has begun to move them. Arabica's requirement of a cool, frost-free mountain climate places it in a band that climbs toward the equator: at 20 degrees of latitude in Brazil it grows at 600 to 1,100 metres, in Central America between 900 and 1,700, near the equator in Colombia, Kenya, and Ethiopia between 1,400 and 2,100. At the poleward edges frost is the standing risk — a severe July frost in Paraná in 1975 removed a large share of Brazilian production within a single night and displaced the industry's centre of gravity north into Minas Gerais — while at the warm edge, rising night temperatures shorten fruit maturation and flatten cup quality, and producing regions from Mesoamerica to East Africa report the suitable band shifting measurably upslope within a working lifetime. Robusta's tolerance of heat gives it the low ground: the great robusta territories are the plains and plateaus of Vietnam — planted at scale after 1986 and now the largest robusta producer — together with lowland Brazil, where the local trade name is conilon, Uganda, whose forests contain the species wild, India, and Indonesia.

The genus's reservoir of adaptation, meanwhile, sits awkwardly with the biology of its seed. Coffee seeds are recalcitrant: they do not survive the drying and freezing on which conventional seed banking depends, so the diversity of the genus is conserved instead in living field collections — rows of trees at CATIE in Costa Rica, at research stations in Ethiopia, Kenya, Cameroon, Madagascar, and India — that are expensive to maintain and exposed to the same weather and disease as any farm, with cryopreservation of embryos still a laboratory-scale supplement. An assessment of the wild species published in 2019 judged some sixty percent of them at risk in the wild, including the forest populations of arabica itself in Ethiopia and South Sudan. The same years produced a demonstration of why the reservoir matters: Coffea stenophylla, a West African species prized in Sierra Leone in the nineteenth century and effectively lost to science for decades, was relocated in the wild in 2018, and blind tastings organized in 2020 found its cup close to a good arabica — from a species that fruits at mean temperatures six degrees warmer than arabica tolerates.

Harvest and the removal of the fruit

A coffee cherry is built in concentric layers: a thin skin, the exocarp; a sweet, sticky pulp and mucilage, the mesocarp, rich in sugars and pectins; a stiff parchment shell, the endocarp; a diaphanous seed coat called silverskin; and, ordinarily, two seeds pressed flat-face to flat-face, each a hemisphere of dense green endosperm folded around an embryo the size of a grain of rice. In five to ten percent of cherries only one ovule develops and the single rounded seed is called a peaberry, sorted out and sold separately in some origins. The object of everything that happens between picking and export is to free the seed from its four wrappings and bring it to a storable dryness without letting anything in the process mark the cup.

Picking is the largest single cost on most farms and the first quality decision. Selective picking — passing through the same trees every week or two across a harvest of several months, taking only red fruit — feeds the washed mills of Colombia, Central America, Kenya, and Ethiopia. Strip picking, in which the whole branch is cleared in one pass by hand or with a hand-held vibrating rake once the majority of fruit is ripe, suits regions with a compressed, uniform ripening. On the flat expanses of the Brazilian cerrado the work is done by self-propelled harvesters that straddle the row and shake the fruit free with rotating fingers, machines whose output is a mixed stream of ripe, unripe, and dried cherries, leaves and twigs, cleaned and classified afterward by winnowing and by flotation in water, where dense ripe fruit sinks and dried or insect-bored fruit floats.

From the receiving tank the fruit takes one of two ancient routes or one of their modern intermediates. The dry route — the natural process — is the older and simpler: whole cherries are spread on sun-drying yards of packed earth, tile, or concrete, or on raised beds of shade netting, in layers a few centimetres deep, raked and turned many times a day, covered against dew and rain, for two to four weeks until the fruit has fallen from around sixty percent moisture to eleven or twelve and rattles like a gourd. The dried husk is later broken off mechanically. The natural process needs no water and little machinery, and where it is done carefully — thin layers, constant turning, quick removal of any fermenting fruit — it yields the fruit-forward cup of the classic Yemeni and Ethiopian styles and of most Brazilian production. Done carelessly, in thick heaps that heat and mould, it yields the flavours that mill managers call ferment, and the difference between the two is nothing but labour and attention.

The wet route — the washed process — was developed to remove the pulp before drying and with it the risk of uncontrolled fermentation inside the fruit. Cherries pass the day they are picked through a pulper, a drum or disc faced with punched metal that squeezes the seeds out of the skin; the pulped seeds, still slippery with a millimetre of adherent mucilage, go into tanks where the pectic layer is left to break down for twelve to seventy-two hours, the time falling with temperature. The agents of that breakdown are the fruit's own pectic enzymes and a dense wild community of lactic acid bacteria and yeasts; the mill's control consists of water, time, and temperature, and the endpoint is read by hand — a batch is done when the seeds, rubbed together, grate cleanly instead of sliding. The loosened mucilage is then washed away, traditionally in long channels where the flow also grades the seeds by density. A newer alternative dispenses with the tank: demucilaging machines strip the layer mechanically between rubber and steel within minutes of pulping, saving water and time at the cost of the fermentation's contribution to the cup. Between the fully washed and the natural lie the intermediates: the pulped natural of Brazil and the honey processes of Central America, in which pulped seeds are dried with some or all of their mucilage still on the parchment — the yellow, red, and black honeys of the trade, named for the darkening of the sugary layer as more of it is left on and dried more slowly.

However the pulp is removed, the seed then dries in its parchment, and the drying is as consequential as anything before it. On patios or raised beds, washed parchment takes one to two weeks to come from fifty-five percent moisture to the target of ten to twelve; mechanical driers — rotating drums or static box driers fed by furnaces burning the mill's own dried husk — do it in a day or two where cloud and harvest volume demand it, held below about forty degrees for the sake of the seed. Drying too fast case-hardens the parchment, sealing moisture inside; drying unevenly, or rewetting overnight, opens the way to mould; and a batch dried below about ten percent loses the bluish-green of fresh seed along with, cuppers report, some of its life. Sumatra's characteristic wet-hulled process, giling basah, breaks the sequence deliberately: parchment is hulled off at thirty to thirty-five percent moisture and the naked seeds finish drying exposed, swelling and darkening as they do, a practice that produces both the region's heavy, low-acid cup and its seeds' characteristic bottle-green colour. Everywhere else the dried seed rests in parchment — reposo, the mills call it — for thirty to sixty days in the warehouse, a rest that measurably stabilizes the seed and evens its moisture before milling.

Milling, grading, and the green trade

Dry milling turns parchment or dried cherry into export-ready green coffee. A huller strips the parchment by friction between a rotating helical screw and a screen; an optional polisher takes off the silverskin; and the stream of naked seed then passes a battery of classifiers. Screens with round holes graded in sixty-fourths of an inch sort by size — screen 18 and above selling in some origins as AA, 15 to 17 as AB and A, the flat-bean nomenclature varying by country; peaberries pass an oblong screen of their own. A gravity separator or densimetric table — a tilted, vibrating, perforated deck through which air is blown — walks dense seed uphill and floats light, insect-bored, or immature seed downhill; and electronic colour sorters, photocells and air jets inspecting the falling stream one seed at a time, eject the blacks, sours, and faded whites that the mechanical stages missed. What the machines cannot remove, hand-sorting along slow belts still does in origins where labour allows it.

The trade then reduces quality to counted numbers. Under the green classification systems in general use, a sample of three hundred or three hundred and fifty grams is picked over and its imperfections tallied against defect equivalents: a full black seed or a cherry stone counting as one full defect, lesser faults — partial blacks, sours, insect damage, broken seeds, floaters, shells — counting in fractions, husk fragments and stones counting by size. A count below a set threshold with no full blacks, together with a clean cup on the sample roast, admits a lot to the specialty grade; national systems in Brazil, Colombia, Kenya, and Ethiopia run parallel scales of type numbers, preparation standards, and auction classes built on the same arithmetic. Moisture at export is specified between ten and twelve percent, and the modern contracts increasingly specify water activity as well, the equilibrium humidity of the seed being a better predictor of storage behaviour than its water content alone. Green coffee ships in the sixty-kilogram jute bag that remains the trade's unit of account — national statistics are kept in millions of bags, and world production in a good year now passes one hundred and seventy million of them, roughly ten million tonnes — increasingly with a food-grade hermetic liner inside the jute, or in bulk, a container's twenty-one tonnes loaded loose onto a liner. Kept cool and dry, well-prepared green coffee holds its quality for a year or more; kept warm and humid, it fades in months toward the flat, papery cup the trade calls past-crop.

The seed that all this care delivers is about half carbohydrate by dry mass, mostly the polysaccharides — arabinogalactans, mannans, cellulose — of its dense cell walls, with around fifteen percent lipids in arabica, eleven percent proteins and free amino acids, six to nine percent chlorogenic acids, five to eight percent sucrose, one percent trigonelline, and the alkaloid caffeine at the species-dependent levels already noted, held in the tissue partly as a complex with the chlorogenic acids. Caffeine itself was first isolated from green coffee in 1819 by the young chemist Friedlieb Ferdinand Runge, working on a packet of beans in Berlin; the compound's role in the living plant is understood as part of its chemical defence, and its concentration varies enough across the genus that Coffea eugenioides and some Malagasy species are nearly free of it. None of the seed's stored chemistry tastes of coffee. The cup is created in the roaster.

The removal of caffeine and the soluble branch

Two industrial branches leave the main chain at the warehouse door. The first takes caffeine out of the green seed. Commercial decaffeination began in Bremen in 1903 with Ludwig Roselius, whose patented process steamed the green seed and drew the alkaloid out with organic solvents; the modern descendants of the method still work on swollen, steamed or water-soaked green coffee, but the industry's growth has been in two gentler processes. In the water process, developed in Switzerland in the 1930s and commercialized at scale in the 1980s, green seed soaks in an extract of green coffee already saturated with everything soluble except caffeine, so that only the caffeine diffuses out along its concentration gradient; the extract is then passed through activated carbon, which strips the caffeine, and returns to the soaking tanks in a closed loop. In the carbon dioxide process, built on Kurt Zosel's work at the Max Planck Institute for coal research in the 1960s and 1970s, carbon dioxide above its critical point — thirty-one degrees and seventy-four bars — behaves as a dense, selective solvent that dissolves caffeine while leaving the seed's larger flavour precursors in place, and releases its cargo when the pressure drops. A third route uses ethyl acetate, an ester present naturally in ripe fruit and, in commercial quantity, derived from fermented sugarcane, which gives the method its trade name. The recovered caffeine is refined and sold into the beverage and pharmaceutical trades, and the decaffeinated seed, its structure slightly opened by the soaking and steaming, roasts faster and paler than untreated coffee. The common regulatory benchmarks call for removal of at least ninety-seven percent of the original caffeine.

The second branch is soluble coffee. Roasted and ground coffee is extracted industrially in trains of percolation columns at temperatures well above brewing — up to 180 degrees under pressure, which hydrolyzes part of the otherwise insoluble cell-wall polysaccharides and roughly doubles the usable yield — and the extract, concentrated by evaporation or by freezing out part of its water, is dried in one of two ways. Spray drying blows a fine mist of concentrate down a tower of hot air, from which the water flashes off in seconds, leaving hollow spherical particles, often agglomerated afterward with steam into the granules of the retail jar. Freeze drying, gentler and costlier, freezes the concentrate into a slab, grinds the slab, and sublimes the ice away under vacuum, preserving more of the volatile aroma; the plants recover volatiles stripped during extraction and fold them back into the concentrate before drying. The product line dates from 1938, when the Nestlé company introduced the first commercially successful spray-dried coffee after some years of development on Brazilian surplus stocks, and soluble manufacture now absorbs a substantial fraction of world robusta production, the species' higher extractable solids suiting the column's economics.

Roasting, grinding, and extraction

Roasting is the application of two to twenty minutes of heat, with the seed passing from room temperature to somewhere between 195 and 230 degrees, and it proceeds through stages a roaster reads by eye, ear, and instrument. In the drying phase the last free water is driven off and the seed yellows; from about 140 degrees the Maillard reactions begin between the seed's reducing sugars and its amino acids, building both colour and hundreds of aroma compounds, joined above 170 by the caramelization of sucrose and the degradation of the chlorogenic acids. Steam and carbon dioxide accumulate in the seed's rigid cells until, at about 196 degrees, the structure yields audibly — first crack, a popping like coarse popcorn — as the seed fractures along its folds and nearly doubles in volume. The interval after first crack is the development time in which the roast's degree is set: stopped soon after, a light roast keeps the origin's acidity and floral or fruit notes; carried toward 210 or 215, sweetness and body build as acids degrade; taken to about 224 degrees the seed cracks a second time, more quietly, as its cell-wall matrix itself begins to pyrolyze, and beyond second crack the oils migrate to the surface and the cup tastes chiefly of the roast. Across the process the seed loses twelve to twenty percent of its weight — water, silverskin, and the carbon dioxide and volatiles of the reactions — and the roaster's quantitative record is the weight loss, the time-temperature curve, and the ground colour of the result measured on near-infrared reflectance scales. The machinery is either a rotating drum over a flame, transferring heat by contact and slow convection, or a fluid bed roasting in a rising column of hot air; the drum's slower transfer suits development of body, the fluid bed's speed suits brightness, and industrial plants roast continuously in tangential and centrifugal designs at throughputs of tonnes per hour. Roasted whole seed then degasses — carbon dioxide equal to several times its volume seeps out over days and weeks — which is why fresh roast is rested before brewing and why the one-way valve on the modern coffee bag exists, venting gas outward while admitting no oxygen inward; staling, when it comes, is the oxidation of the seed's lipids and the escape of its volatiles, rapid once the seed is ground.

Perhaps eight hundred to a thousand volatile compounds have been identified in roasted coffee — furans, pyrazines, aldehydes, ketones, sulphur compounds, the last including some of the most potent aroma contributors known — together with the melanoidins, the brown nitrogenous polymers of Maillard chemistry that carry much of the cup's body and colour. Extraction is the transfer of the soluble fraction of this chemistry into water, and its arithmetic is simple: roasted coffee is at most about thirty percent soluble, palatable extraction takes eighteen to twenty-two percent of the grounds' mass into the cup, and the strength of the beverage is then fixed by the ratio of water to coffee — around sixty grams per litre for filter brewing, giving a beverage of 1.2 to 1.5 percent dissolved solids, against a ratio of roughly two to one and a strength near ten percent in espresso, where water at nine bars of pressure is forced through a compacted bed of fine grounds in twenty-five to thirty seconds, emulsifying oils and suspending fines into a texture no percolation method reaches, capped by the crema of gas-supersaturated extract. Between the percolation and immersion families — the paper-filtered cone and batch brewer on one side, the French press and the cupping bowl on the other — the variables are five: the fineness of the grind, set by the spacing of burr mills whose particle distributions are themselves measured by laser diffraction; the temperature of the water, conventionally 90 to 96 degrees; the contact time; the agitation; and the water itself, whose dissolved minerals buffer the cup's acids strongly enough that the professional associations publish target ranges for hardness and alkalinity. Under-extraction leaves the acids dominant and the cup sour and thin; over-extraction drags in the slow-dissolving bitter fraction; and the industry's sensory bench-mark for judging all of it remains the cupping table — five or more bowls per lot, a standard eight and a quarter grams per hundred and fifty millilitres of water, the crust broken and the aroma taken at four minutes, the liquor slurped loudly from a spoon at eight — a protocol little changed in a century of use, by which a trained palate scores fragrance, flavour, aftertaste, acidity, body, balance, uniformity, and cleanness, and by which the whole chain described here, from the chromosomes of a highland shrub to the mineral content of the brewing water, is finally held to account.
